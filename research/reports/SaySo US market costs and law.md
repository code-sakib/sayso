# America Pays SaySo More, Sues Faster

SaySo works in the United States as a business, and the US is a better market than India on almost every number except legal risk. The reachable market is about **95,000 high-ticket, advice-led stores** (car dealers, jewellers, opticians, mattress, furniture, wireless). At the price this report recommends, **$200 per store plus $60 per badge per month**, that is roughly a **$640 million a year** serviceable market, 12–20 times India's. A typical 8-badge US store costs about **$250–300 a month to run on the default Meta stack**, so an $680 bill earns about **56–63% gross margin**. Self-hosting speech-to-text lifts that to about **75–87%**. The price sits far below the US in-person coaching leaders, Rilla and Siro, which charge roughly **$200–420 per rep per month**. It is also only about 3% of a typical store's frontline payroll, against 25–32% in India, so the buyer math is much easier. The catch is law. US recording law is a state-by-state patchwork. About a dozen states require every party's consent to record, Illinois adds the BIPA biometric law with per-person damages, and a 2025 California ruling (*Ambriz v. Google*) lets plaintiffs sue an AI vendor directly as an "eavesdropper." Staff-only mode cuts much of that risk but does not remove the need to tell customers they are being recorded. The recommendation is to **launch in one-party-consent states such as Texas, Georgia, Arizona, Ohio and North Carolina, then New York and New Jersey, and leave California and Illinois for last**, after US counsel signs off. Every cost and price line marked "estimate" is arithmetic on sourced unit prices, not a published figure, and nothing here is legal advice.

Two demand signals help. Two findings matter most. First, no product, patent or vendor map found anywhere joins in-store audio coaching to CCTV walkout detection ([Borentis map](https://borentis.com/learn/in-store-conversation-intelligence-vendors-map/)). Three traps would wreck the margin. ## Wiretap law, not price, decides where SaySo launches

US law on recording is set mostly by states. Federal law needs only one party's consent and covers audio, not silent video ([National Law Review/Ogletree](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-surveillance-tools-retail-stores-pose-legal-risks)). The salesperson is a party, so in one-party states the retailer can supply consent through its employees. In "all-party" states every person recorded, including the customer, must consent.

| State group | States | What SaySo needs |
|---|---|---|
| All-party consent for audio | California, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Washington ([NLR/Ogletree](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-surveillance-tools-retail-stores-pose-legal-risks)) | Customer notice and arguably consent: entrance signs, badge light, spoken line |
| Special cases | Oregon (all-party or notice for in-person talk); Nevada (court reads phone calls as all-party); Connecticut (all-party for phone, one-party in person) ([RCFP](https://www.rcfp.org/introduction-to-reporters-recording-guide/)) | Treat as all-party to be safe, except Connecticut in-store |
| One-party consent | Most other states, e.g. Texas, Georgia, Arizona, Ohio, North Carolina, Virginia, New York, New Jersey, Colorado (verify each) | Employee written consent and notice; customer signage still recommended |

California is the riskiest all-party state. Its law protects in-person "confidential" talk, judged by whether a person reasonably expects not to be recorded ([Flanagan v. Flanagan](https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/flanagan-v-flanagan-32179)). Busy shop-floor chat may fall outside that, but a quiet conversation about financing or health may not. Statutory damages are widely cited at $5,000 per violation, though that figure was not re-verified. The September 2026 California reform, SB 690, does not help: it dropped the business-purpose exemption and leaves in-person recording claims intact ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20261006-cipa-reform-signed-into-law)).

The second risk is new and aimed at vendors. In *Ambriz v. Google* (February 2025), a court let a class action proceed against Google as a third-party eavesdropper on customer calls. The court held that a vendor counts as a separate listener if it merely *could* use the data for itself, and Google's terms permitting AI training were enough. It also said a business-to-business contract cannot replace the consumer's own consent ([ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/privacy/federal-judge-allows-google-customer-service-ai-class-action-to-proceed/)). The same theory drives the consolidated Otter.ai notetaker suits ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/dressing-old-laws-in-class-action-suits-applying-anti-wiretapping-laws-to-ai-transcription-services)) and the Patagonia/Talkdesk case ([The Record](https://therecord.media/patagonia-sued-privacy-invasion-california)). SaySo's defence is to make secondary use impossible, both in its contract terms and in its engineering: no training on retailer data, no product improvement on raw audio, and customer voice never leaving the badge.

Biometric law is the third risk. Illinois's BIPA lets individuals sue for **$1,000 per negligent and $5,000 per reckless violation**. A 2024 amendment now counts damages per person rather than per scan, and an appeals court held in April 2026 that this applies to pending cases ([Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/data-privacy/seventh-circuit-holds-that-bipa-amendment-applies-retroactively/)). Voiceprints are covered but undefined. In *Delgado v. Meta* (2026), a court let a voiceprint claim continue even though Meta said it built no speaker template ([Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202608/meta-loses-bid-to-end-bipa-voiceprint-suit-as-case-moves-forward)). That matters because staff-only mode most likely works by enrolling each salesperson's voice, which creates a stored template tied to a named employee. Employees can sign a written biometric release at onboarding, but the customer's voice must never be templated. On the video side, Target faced a BIPA suit that survived dismissal although Target denied using face recognition ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2024/11/federal-court-allows-illegal-data-collection-claims-against-target-to-move-forward)), so SaySo's camera module should use short-lived track IDs, no face crops and no cross-visit matching, and say so publicly. Colorado added employee biometric consent rules on 1 July 2025, enforced only by the state attorney general ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/new-colorado-law-imposes-heightened-requirements-for-the-collection-and-processing-of-biometric-data)). Texas has a biometric law enforced by its attorney general, with large settlements against Meta and Google, though those details were not verified in this research.

Staff-only mode helps a lot, but not everywhere. The table sums up what it fixes.

| Risk | Does staff-only mode fix it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| SaySo sued as a vendor "eavesdropper" | **Mostly yes** | If customer audio never reaches SaySo's servers, there is nothing for SaySo to misuse |
| Customer voiceprint claims under BIPA | **Mostly yes** | No customer template is ever stored |
| Breach and retention harm | **Yes** | No customer audio or transcript exists to leak |
| All-party wiretap law | **Only partly** | The badge still hears the customer for a moment before deleting, which may count as eavesdropping or interception; **notice is still needed** ([NLR/Ogletree](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-surveillance-tools-retail-stores-pose-legal-risks)) |
| Employee obligations | **No** | Staff audio is still recorded, so employee notice and consent remain |

Employee monitoring rules apply everywhere SaySo deploys. New York requires written notice and acknowledgment of electronic monitoring, with penalties of $500–3,000 ([MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/211115-new-york-enacts-employee-monitoring-notification-law)). Delaware has a similar rule, and Connecticut rewrote its law effective 1 October 2026 ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/deadline-imminent-connecticuts-expanded-electronic-monitoring-law)). California's privacy regulator approved rules on automated decision-making in September 2025. From 2027 they require notice, explanation and generally an opt-out or human appeal when such tools drive employment decisions such as discipline or pay ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/08/cppa-board-finalizes-new-rules-on-admt-cybersecurity-audits-and-risk-assessments)). So SaySo scores should coach, and any discipline should require human review. Federal labour pressure eased when the NLRB's general counsel withdrew the 2022 memo targeting workplace surveillance in February 2025 ([Kilpatrick Townsend](https://ktslaw.com/insights/alert/2025/2/reshaping-federal-labor-policy-acting-nlrb-general-counsel-rescinds-numerous-biden-era-guidance-memos)), but recording union talk is still risky and a future board could reverse course.

The FTC is the national backstop. Its Rite Aid order banned the chain from facial recognition for five years after thousands of false "match" alerts that staff acted on ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/82185); [Mayer Brown](https://mayerbrown.com/de/insights/publications/2023/12/more-than-a-ban-on-facial-recognition-use-the-us--ftcs-rite-aid-action-and-proposed-stipulated-order)). For SaySo this means two things. Walkout alerts must never label a person a shoplifter and must go to a human for review. And every marketing promise, such as "customer voice is deleted in seconds," becomes an enforceable claim, so it must be technically true and ideally checked inside the SOC 2 audit.

**Recommended launch order (planning judgement, not legal advice)**

| Phase | States | Reason | Extra step |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Launch** | **Texas, Georgia, Arizona, Ohio, North Carolina** | One-party consent; large retail markets; no private-lawsuit biometric law | In Texas, get written employee consent for voice enrolment |
| **1b. Add for density** | **New York, New Jersey** | One-party consent; dense jewellery and dealer markets | New York's written employee-monitoring notice |
| 2. With counsel sign-off | Florida, Pennsylvania, Washington, Massachusetts, Maryland, Michigan | All-party consent | Signs, badge light, spoken notice; state-specific opinion |
| 3. Last | **California** | All-party consent, high damages, home court of the *Ambriz* vendor theory | Full legal review; CCPA service-provider terms; risk assessment |
| 3. Last | **Illinois** | All-party consent plus BIPA private lawsuits | BIPA releases, public retention schedule |

One caution limits this plan. No US court has ruled on a wearable audio badge in a store, or on whether brief on-device "capture then delete" processing counts as eavesdropping. The one-party state list also comes from secondary sources and needs checking against each state's statute before contracts are signed.

The pattern is clear. The US pays about three times more per store and has a market roughly 15 times bigger, while costs are only about 2.5 times higher, so margins come out slightly better. India's risk is a single national regulator and a 2027 deadline. America's risk is private lawsuits, which arrive faster, cost more to defend and can target SaySo directly, not only its retail customer.

## Conclusion

The US changes which problem SaySo has to solve first. In India the hard question was whether stores could afford it. In the US affordability is close to settled, since $680 a month is small next to an 8-person payroll and a fraction of Rilla's price. The binding constraints become legal design and trust paperwork. Staff-only mode is worth more in the US than in India because it attacks the specific theory, the vendor as eavesdropper, that has put Google and Otter.ai in court. It only works as a defence if it is real in the engineering (customer audio never leaves the badge, no customer templates), real in the contract (no training, service-provider terms) and verified by an outside auditor.

The practical sequence follows from that. Build the consent kit, deletion logging and service-provider contract before the first US pilot, and start a SOC 2 Type I immediately, because the six-month observation window is the longest lead time on the list. Assemble badges outside China. Then run a paid pilot with a Texas or Georgia jewellery or mattress operator to produce the same three numbers India needs: real speech-hours per badge, customer opt-out rate, and conversion lift against control stores. A fourth number, the share of customers who object to a badge light in a one-party state, will show whether the all-party states are a legal problem or also a market one.
